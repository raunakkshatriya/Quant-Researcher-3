PAIRS TRADING (statistical arbitrage) - two universes, one engine
=================================================================
Run:  python step5_pairs.py          (train period only; test period locked)
      python step5_pairs.py --test   (one logged look at the test period, both universes)

WHAT IT DOES, IN PLAIN WORDS
Pick two stocks (or funds) from the same corner of the market that tend to move together. When one
becomes unusually expensive compared with the other, SHORT the expensive one and BUY the cheap one.
When the gap closes again, close both. We are betting on the GAP, not on the market going up, so
market ups and downs mostly cancel out. Each pair is ONE position made of two legs.

THE PROCESS, STEP BY STEP
1. GROUPS     Pairs are only formed INSIDE a group (our economic reason for two names to be linked).
2. FORM       On the first trading day of every calendar year, take the previous 504 trading days
              (about 2 years) of prices. For each pair inside a group:
                a) hedge ratio beta from regressing log(price A) on log(price B)
                b) Engle-Granger cointegration test: does the gap  log(A) - beta*log(B)  keep returning
                   to an average? (statsmodels `coint`, maxlag 5)
                c) half-life: how many days the gap takes to close halfway
3. KEEP       Keep pairs with p-value < 0.05, beta between 0.2 and 5, half-life between 3 and 60 days.
              Rank by p-value, take the best 8, each ticker used in at most one pair.
4. TRADE      For the rest of the year, each day compute the gap's z-score (how many standard
              deviations from its own 60-day average):
                z <= -entry_z : gap too low  -> BUY A, SHORT B
                z >= +entry_z : gap too high -> SHORT A, BUY B
                close when z returns to exit_z (0 or 0.5)
                STOP: if |z| > 4 the relationship is probably broken: close and stay out until z is
                back inside +-entry_z.
5. SIZE       Each of the 8 slots gets 1/8 of our money (gross). Inside a slot, A gets 1/(1+beta) and B gets
              beta/(1+beta), so the two legs hedge each other. Empty slots stay in cash (earning T-bills).
              Pre-trade risk limits: max 25% in any one ticker, max 100% gross.
6. RESET      On the first trading day of the next year everything is closed and the pairs are re-chosen
              using only the data available by then (no look-ahead).
7. EXECUTION  Signals use the close of day t; trades happen at the close of t+1; first return earned day t+2.
8. COSTS      Stocks: 5 bp per $ traded per leg. ETFs: 3 bp. Short borrow fee 25 bp a year on the short leg.
              (Costs differ between universes on purpose - ETFs really are cheaper to trade - so part of any
              difference in results is cost, not idea. The test-period cost table shows the sensitivity.)

SETTINGS THE LAB TRIES (train period only): entry_z in {1.5, 2.0, 2.5}  x  exit_z in {0.0, 0.5} = 6 combinations.
Fixed on purpose (changing them is a new version): 504-day formation, 60-day z window, 8 pairs, stop at 4.

BENCHMARK: cash (the T-bill rate). A market-neutral strategy is not compared with SPY; the question is
whether it earns more than cash. The comparison script also shows SPY by year and market regime, for context.

KNOWN WEAKNESSES (read these before believing any result)
* Multiple testing: we test ~130 (stocks) or 55 (ETFs) pairs every year. At p < 0.05 about 5% pass by pure
  luck. The comparison output prints how many passed vs how many luck alone would pass.
* Survivorship: the stock list is today's giants. Pairs where one company later failed are missing.
* Pairs break (mergers, scandals, technology shifts). The stop limits the damage, not the cost.
* Crowded trade: large funds do this with better data and lower costs.
* Low volatility: with 1/8 slots and often fewer than 8 pairs, the strategy holds little risk and mostly cash.
  Do NOT "fix" that by adding leverage until the edge itself is proven.
