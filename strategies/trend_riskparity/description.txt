CROSS-ASSET TREND FOLLOWING + INVERSE-VOLATILITY SIZING - what it does and how
==============================================================================

THE IDEA
Asset prices tend to trend: an asset that has risen over the past 6-12 months is, on average,
more likely to keep rising for a while than to fall, and the reverse. This "time-series momentum"
shows up across stocks, bonds, currencies and commodities (Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen, 2012) and
is the basis of managed-futures (CTA) funds. Two ideas are combined here:
  1. Trend filter: hold an asset only while its own trend is up; otherwise sit in cash.
     (Unlike S1/S3, this compares each asset with ITS OWN past, not with other assets.)
  2. Risk-balanced sizing: a calm asset (bonds) should get more money than a wild one (stocks),
     so no single asset dominates the portfolio's risk.
The hope is smoother returns and shallower drawdowns than a plain stock portfolio, not a higher
return in good markets.

WHAT WE TRADE (our specific execution)
Nine ETFs, one per asset class (all exist at different dates):
  SPY  US large-cap stocks                 EFA  Developed-market stocks outside the US (from 2001)
  EEM  Emerging-market stocks (2003)       TLT  Long-term US government bonds (2002)
  IEF  Medium-term US government bonds (2002)   LQD  Investment-grade corporate bonds (2002)
  GLD  Gold (2004)                         DBC  Broad commodities (2006)
  VNQ  US real estate / REITs (2004)
We do not trade until at least 4 of the funds have enough history, so the first trades happen
around 2003-2004. Before DBC (2006) the portfolio simply has fewer choices.
Benchmark for comparison: a 60/40 portfolio, 60% SPY and 40% IEF, rebalanced daily (simplified).
A pure stock benchmark would be unfair: this strategy holds bonds and gold too.

THE RULES
On the last trading day of every month:
  1. Uptrend test: asset price today > asset price L trading days ago? If not, weight 0.
  2. Among assets in an uptrend, weight = (1 / recent volatility), where volatility is the
     standard deviation of daily returns over the last 60 days, annualised. Normalise to 100%.
  3. Estimate the portfolio's volatility from the last 60 days of returns of the held assets
     (including how they move together). If it exceeds the target T (e.g. 8% a year), multiply
     every weight by T / estimate. Never multiply by more than 1: NO LEVERAGE.
  4. Unused money stays in cash.
Long only. No shorting.

THE SETTINGS L AND T
L in {126, 189, 252} trading days (6, 9, 12 months); T in {6%, 8%, 10%}: 9 combinations.
Each is scored on TRAIN only (start of data to 2025-12-31). The runner reports the best, its
deflated Sharpe probability, and a walk-forward score. The TEST period (2026-01-01 onwards)
stays locked until you run with --test.

HOW THE MONEY IS CALCULATED
- Signal uses the close of day t; trade at the close of day t+1; the new holdings first earn
  day t+2's return.
- Cost: 3 basis points per dollar traded (liquid ETFs). A cost-sensitivity table is printed
  with --test.
- Cash: money not invested earns the 3-month US Treasury bill rate (FRED series DTB3), using the
  previous day's published rate. Sharpe ratios are computed on returns in EXCESS of that cash
  rate. (Set USE_CASH_YIELD = False in config.py to see the old 0% assumption.) If the rate
  cannot be downloaded the program says so and falls back to 0%.
- $100 start on the first day the strategy holds something.

HOW TO JUDGE THE RESULTS
- Compare with the 60/40 portfolio. Win on risk (smaller drawdowns), not necessarily on return.
- Look at the worst drawdown: trend following is meant to cut it, especially in 2008 and 2022.
- Trend following loses money in choppy, trendless markets (e.g. 2011-2013, early 2026?):
  long stretches of "whipsaw" are normal, not a sign of a bug.

KNOWN LIMITATIONS
- Only about 20 years of history, and only a couple of bear markets (2008, 2022): the sample
  for judging a defensive strategy is tiny.
- Bond funds had a 40-year tailwind from falling interest rates; trend in bonds looked great
  partly for that reason, which may not repeat.
- ETFs, not futures: real managed-futures funds trade futures with leverage and shorting.
- Cash yield is the T-bill rate, not what your broker pays you (often lower). Monthly rebalancing may react late to fast moves.
- Weights between month-ends are assumed constant (no drift cost).
